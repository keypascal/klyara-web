### VIDEO 1 — "Making Tax Digital for Builders: Are You In?" (target 7 min)

> **[0:00 — HOOK, no intro, straight in]**
>
> If you're self-employed in the building trade, there's a decent chance HMRC changed your
> obligations in April and nobody told you. This video tells you in seven minutes whether it applies
> to you and what you actually have to do. No intro, no jargon.
>
> **[0:38 — WHO'S IN]**
>
> Making Tax Digital for Income Tax. It started in April 2026, and it applies to sole traders and
> landlords with qualifying income over fifty thousand pounds.
>
> April 2027, that threshold drops to thirty thousand. April 2028, twenty thousand.
>
> So even if you're out now, look at the year after next, because a lot of people reading twenty
> grand as a low bar for a building firm are going to find they're in it by 2028.
>
> HMRC looks at your qualifying income from the tax year *two years* before. So for the April 2026
> start, they're looking at your 2024/25 figures. The ones you already filed.
>
> **[2:05 — THE GROSS TRAP]**
>
> Here's the part that catches subcontractors, and I want to be really clear about it because I've
> had this conversation a dozen times in the last month.
>
> Qualifying income means your gross self-employment income plus your gross property income, added
> together. Gross. Before expenses. And before CIS deductions.
>
> So say you invoiced fifty-five thousand pounds last year. Twenty percent CIS came off, that's
> eleven grand. You banked forty-four.
>
> HMRC counts fifty-five. You're over the threshold. You're in MTD. And your bank statement spent the
> whole year telling you that you weren't.
>
> If you're around that line, go and look at your gross invoices, not your bank.
>
> **[3:20 — WHAT A QUARTERLY UPDATE IS]**
>
> Now, what you actually have to do — and this is less frightening than people think.
>
> A quarterly update is a summary of your income and expenses for that three-month period, sent from
> compatible software. That's it. It's totals by category.
>
> It is *not* a tax return. It's not a tax calculation. You don't pay anything on it. Nothing is
> final. You can correct it later.
>
> What you do need is digital records — meaning your income and expenses recorded digitally as you go,
> not typed up in one go in January from a carrier bag.
>
> That's genuinely the whole change. Same information, kept as you go, summarised four times a year.
>
> **[4:41 — THE FOUR DATES]**
>
> The standard quarters end on the fifth of July, the fifth of October, the fifth of January and the
> fifth of April.
>
> The updates are due on the seventh of August, the seventh of November, the seventh of February and
> the seventh of May.
>
> One month and two days after each quarter closes. Every time.
>
> You can elect to use calendar quarters instead — end of June, September, December, March. The
> deadlines don't change, it just makes the bookkeeping tidier if your records run to month end.
> Most builders should take that option.
>
> **[5:35 — FINAL DECLARATION]**
>
> Then at the end of the year there's the final declaration. That's where you confirm everything,
> claim your reliefs, add any other income, and get your actual tax figure.
>
> Deadline: thirty-first of January. Same as it's always been. And you pay on the same date too.
>
> So the annual bit hasn't gone. It's just got four checkpoints in front of it now.
>
> **[6:10 — WHAT IF YOU DON'T]**
>
> HMRC runs a points-based penalty system for late submissions. Miss enough of them and you get a
> financial penalty, and separate late-payment penalties sit on top of that.
>
> But honestly, the penalties aren't the real cost. The real cost is doing four times the panic
> instead of one. If your records aren't running properly, MTD doesn't give you one bad week in
> January — it gives you four bad weeks a year, forever.
>
> **[6:48 — WHAT TO DO THIS WEEK]**
>
> Three things.
>
> One: look at your gross invoices for 2024/25 and work out if you're over fifty thousand.
>
> Two: if you are, get compatible software and connect your bank feed. Connected, feeding
> automatically — not exported and typed in.
>
> Three: put the four dates in your phone now, with a week's warning on each.
>
> That's it. Free guide in the description, fifteen pages, no email needed. And if you want to know
> whether you're in scope and you'd rather just ask a human, the number's down there too.

### VIDEO 2 — "The VAT Domestic Reverse Charge, Explained Once and Properly" (target 9 min)

> **[0:00]** Five years this has been law, and I still see it invoiced wrong every week. So here it
> is, properly, in nine minutes. If you invoice construction work to another business, this affects
> what goes on your invoice and how much cash is in your account.
>
> **[0:45 — WHAT IT DOES]** Normally you charge VAT, you collect it, you hand it to HMRC at the
> quarter end. Under the reverse charge you don't charge it at all. Your customer accounts for both
> sides of the VAT themselves. Same tax, different person handling it. HMRC brought it in to stop
> missing-trader fraud in construction — someone charging VAT, collecting it, and disappearing.
>
> **[1:50 — THE FIVE TESTS]** All five must be true. One: the supply is construction work within the
> CIS definition. Two: both of you are VAT registered. Three: both of you are registered for CIS.
> Four: the customer is not an end user. Five: it's standard or reduced rated — zero-rated work,
> like a new build, is out.
>
> All five yes, reverse charge. Any one no, charge VAT as normal.
>
> **[4:10 — END USERS]** This is where it goes wrong. An end user is the person who actually uses the
> finished building rather than selling the work on. So a property developer building to sell — end
> user, charge VAT. A main contractor who'll invoice your work onward as part of their job — not an
> end user, reverse charge applies.
>
> Crucially, the customer has to tell you they're an end user, in writing. It's their declaration,
> not your guess. Get it in an email at the start of the job and keep it, because if it's wrong,
> HMRC's first question is what you were told and whether you kept it.
>
> **[5:30 — THE INVOICE]** No VAT amount charged. You still show the VAT rate that would have applied,
> or the amount of VAT the customer needs to account for. And you need a clear statement — something
> like: "Reverse charge: VAT Act 1994 Section 55A applies. Customer to account to HMRC for the reverse
> charge output tax on the VAT-exclusive price of items marked reverse charge."
>
> Put it on the template once. Never think about it again.
>
> **[6:40 — YOUR VAT RETURN]** As the supplier: the net sale goes in box 6. No output tax in box 1,
> because you didn't charge any. As the customer: you put the VAT you're accounting for in box 1,
> reclaim the same amount in box 4 if you're entitled to, and the net purchase goes in box 7.
> For most customers it nets to nothing in cash terms. It's a bookkeeping entry.
>
> **[7:50 — THE CASH FLOW BIT]** Here's what nobody warned anyone about. Before this, you collected
> VAT on your sales and held it till the quarter end. It was never your money, but it was in your
> account, and a lot of firms were quietly using it as working capital.
>
> That float has gone. And if most of your sales are now reverse charged while you're still paying VAT
> on materials, you may be in a permanent repayment position — HMRC owes you every quarter.
>
> If that's you, apply to switch to monthly VAT returns. You get the money twelve times a year instead
> of four. I've seen firms sit on quarterly for two years wondering why cash is always tight.
>
> **[8:40 — THREE MISTAKES]** One: assuming it applies to everything, and reverse-charging an end
> user. Two: reclaiming VAT a subcontractor charged you when they shouldn't have — you can't, send it
> back. Three: never getting the end user declaration in writing.
>
> Free checker in the description.

## If you want AI presenter videos (HeyGen)

The Build Manual already budgets HeyGen + ElevenLabs at ~£25/mo. **My recommendation: don't use an AI
avatar as the face of Klyara.** The whole positioning is "a real construction specialist, not a call
centre" — an obviously synthetic presenter undercuts that, and UK trades audiences are unusually
quick to call it out.

Use them for what they're genuinely good at:
- **Voiceover only** (ElevenLabs) over the kinetic typography videos, where there's no claimed face
- **Client-facing explainer videos inside the portal** — "how to snap a receipt", "how to read your
  P&L" — where nobody expects a personality
- **The website's how-it-works video**, if Craig genuinely won't go on camera

For social, a 30-second phone video of Craig in a van beats a polished avatar every time. That's not
a style preference, it's what converts in this market.

## Thumbnail rule for YouTube

Four words maximum, in Bricolage 800, on pine, with one brass word. Face optional
but helps. Never use a red arrow and a shocked face — it's wrong for a financial services brand and
the audience distrusts it.

Working thumbnail lines:
```
ARE YOU IN?          (brass: IN)
20% OR 30%?          (brass: 30%)
COUNT YOUR RETENTIONS (brass: COUNT)
ALWAYS SKINT?        (brass: SKINT)
```
