Decision: build it yourself, not in Lovable

Chapter one · The four questions you asked

Straight answers first.

Your read on the plan is right in shape. Two details in it are wrong, one number is optimistic, and there is one trap that will bite you in the first ten minutes if nobody warns you about it.

"It's a web app, not a Play Store or App Store app — and we do that because it's easier, then do the real apps later if it works."

Correct, and it's the right order. One thing to adjust in your head though: on Android a well-built installable web app is functionally the same thing as a native app — own icon, own storage, push notifications, offline. It is not a compromise. iOS is where the gap is real, and it's narrow: your client has to install it manually from the share sheet before push notifications work at all.

So "do the real apps later" is really "do the iPhone app later, if iPhone clients ever complain." Chapter 8 has the ladder and what each rung costs.

"Are we tied and locked into Lovable, or is it just a build we host ourselves somewhere cheaper?"

Lovable itself does not lock you in. Lovable Cloud does — and it switches itself on by default.

Lovable writes an ordinary React app into a GitHub repo you own. You can clone it, run it, and deploy it to Netlify or Cloudflare for £0. Nothing proprietary ships in that code. That half is genuinely clean.

The trap is the back end. The moment you ask Lovable for anything involving a login or a database, it silently spins up Lovable Cloud — a Supabase instance held inside Lovable's account. You get no Supabase dashboard, no service-role key, no direct database URL, and the billing runs through Lovable's credits. There is a "remove" button but no migration path, and getting off it later costs you every user's password and every user ID in your database.

Fix: connect your own Supabase project before your very first back-end prompt. Chapter 4 spells out the exact order.

"I'm worried about AI costs and Lovable build costs. Is there a better platform? Is this the right way to do this?"

The worry is justified and my answer is: skip Lovable and build it here, with me, against the same Supabase. Same stack, same output, same five-day timeline — and the tooling line on your P&L goes to zero because you already pay for it.

Lovable's meter is the specific problem. A credit is roughly a prompt, Pro gives you 100 a month for $25, and the money doesn't go on building — it goes on fixing. One documented authentication bug ate 15 credits. Someone else burned a whole month's allowance in one afternoon on a Stripe integration. Those are exactly the two jobs on your build list.

And the deciding factor is you: you're technically capable, you hate vendor lock-in, and the design system is already fixed and extracted from klyara.com. Lovable's real advantage is a visual editor for people who can't read the code — you'd be paying for a feature you don't need. Chapter 5 is the full route.

What I'd change in the build plan

Correction 1 · The budget line is light

The manual budgets £25–45/month for "Lovable + Supabase". Realistic on the Lovable route is £45–90/month during the build weeks ($25–100 Lovable, plus Supabase). And within two or three months, receipt photos push you past Supabase's free storage tier onto Pro at $25/month regardless of which route you take — so put a permanent £20/month Supabase line in the model, not £0.

Correction 2 · Two different payment systems are being conflated

The manual has GoCardless Direct Debit for monthly retainers (correct — it's what UK practices use, and it's ~1% capped £4 against 2.8% + 20p for a business card). It separately has "Stripe checkout in the add-on store" at weeks 6–12. Those are two different integrations for two different jobs. Don't let one get built as if it replaces the other. Chapter 7.

What the plan already gets right — don't touch these

PWA before native. Supabase in the EU/London region, set at creation. Row-level security on from prompt one. The email-forwarding bridge to Xero/FreeAgent/QuickBooks instead of OAuth integrations in week one — that single decision saves you about three weeks. Ship ugly by day five. All correct.

The one-line answer

Build a React + Supabase progressive web app, hosted on Cloudflare Pages at app.klyara.com, written here rather than in Lovable, with your own Supabase project in London from minute one. It installs to a phone home screen like an app, it costs about £20/month to run, and every part of it is portable.

Chapter three · How it actually connects

Site, app, login, database, receipts.

Four connections do all the work. None of them is complicated, and one of them is the entire security model.

1 · The website and the app are two separate things

klyara.com stays exactly as it is: a static site served by a Cloudflare Worker out of the keypascal/klyara-web repo. The portal is a completely separate application on a subdomain, in the same Cloudflare account. They are not integrated, and they don't need to be.

NameDNS recordPoints atWhat lives there
klyara.comCloudflare custom domainThe klyara-web WorkerMarketing pages, blog, tools, lead magnets Live
www.klyara.comCloudflare custom domainThe same WorkerSame site Live
app.klyara.comCloudflare custom domainThe portal’s Pages projectLogin, receipts, documents, admin Does not exist yet

The "Client login" button in the site header becomes an ordinary link to https://app.klyara.com. That's the whole integration. No shared sessions, no single sign-on, no plugin. It takes about ten minutes.

Two things about the live site to fix, checked 27 Aug 2026

app.klyara.com does not exist. klyara.com and www.klyara.com both resolve to Cloudflare and serve the Worker. There is no DNS record of any kind on app — nothing answers there. Nothing is broken; it simply has not been created. Attaching the custom domain to the portal's Cloudflare Pages project creates the record and the certificate for you, because Cloudflare already runs the zone.

The "Client login" button in the header points at login.html — the hidden control-panel gate, which is for you and Craig, not for clients. Anyone clicking it today lands on an internal login. Repoint it at app.klyara.com the moment the portal is live, and until then point it at the contact page or hide it.

2 · Authentication — who someone is

  1. Someone signs up

    Email and password go straight to Supabase Auth. Your app never sees or stores the password.

  2. Supabase creates the user, a database trigger creates their profile

    One row in auth.users (Supabase's own table), one row in your profiles table with their name, company, phone and role — client or admin.

  3. The browser gets a signed token

    A JWT, valid for an hour, refreshed silently. It sits in the browser and gets attached to every single request the app makes.

  4. Postgres reads that token and decides what they can see

    This is the part that matters. Read the next section twice.

3 · Row-level security — the whole security model

Your app talks to the database directly from the browser, using a public key. That sounds alarming and it is fine — but only because of row-level security. RLS is a rule written on each table that says, in effect: "you may only see rows where client_id equals the user ID in the token you just presented." Postgres enforces it. There is no way around it from the browser.

This is the single biggest failure mode of AI-built apps

If RLS is off, or a policy is written loosely, the public key in your JavaScript bundle — which anyone can read in DevTools — will happily return every row of every table. Every publicised vibe-coded data breach is this exact bug.

You are holding builders' receipts, ID documents and financial records. So: RLS enabled on every table from the first migration, policies written as SQL in files you have read, and a test that logs in as client A and confirms client B's receipts return nothing. Never let a tool add a table without you reading its policy.

The two keys, and the rule about them

KeyWhere it livesPower
anon keyIn the browser bundle. Public. Fine.Only what RLS policies allow for that logged-in user.
service-role keyEdge Functions only. Never in the front end. Never in git.Bypasses RLS entirely. Reads and writes everything.

4 · The receipt journey, end to end

  1. Dave photographs a diesel receipt in the portal

    An <input type="file" capture> opens the phone camera. The image is shrunk in the browser to roughly 300 KB before it uploads — this matters, both for site signal and for your storage bill.

  2. It lands in Supabase Storage under his folder

    Private bucket, path receipts/<client_id>/<uuid>.jpg. A row goes into the receipts table with status new. Dave sees the chip flip to "Received".

  3. Craig sees it in the review queue

    Admin-only screen. Approve or reject with a reason. This is the two-minutes-a-day routine the whole product is built around.

  4. An Edge Function emails it into the client's books

    On approval, the function reads that client's platform_inbox_email — their QuickBooks receipt-forwarding address, their Xero email-to-Files address, or their FreeAgent auto-extract inbox — and sends the file there through Brevo. Status flips to sent, Dave's chip reads "In your books".

  5. Xero, FreeAgent or QuickBooks extracts it as normal

    Their OCR, their workflow, their problem. You wrote no integration code.

Why this design is worth defending

Switching a client's back end — from QuickBooks receipt capture to Dext, say, at client 12 — is changing one email address in the admin panel. The client sees nothing. Building real OAuth integrations in week one would have cost you three weeks and locked the same behaviour behind three times the code. Do them at month three, QuickBooks first, once clients are paying.

5 · What "it's an app on my phone" actually means

A web app manifest file plus a service worker turns the site into an installable one. Dave taps Share → Add to Home Screen on an iPhone, or gets a proper install prompt on Android. He then has a Klyara icon, a full-screen app with no browser chrome, and a splash screen. It is indistinguishable from a downloaded app to him. It cost you about half a day.

Chapter four · The tutorial you asked for

Lovable, honestly explained.

Even though I'd steer you elsewhere, you should understand it properly — partly to make the decision yourself, partly because the setup order below is what saves you if you do use it.

What it is, mechanically

A chat box next to a live preview of your app. You describe what you want, it writes and edits files, the preview refreshes, you look, you describe the next thing. Under the hood it produces an entirely ordinary front end — Vite + React + TypeScript + Tailwind + shadcn/ui + React Router, the same stack a developer would pick by hand — and connects it to Supabase for the back end. There is no proprietary runtime. That's why the exit is clean, if you set it up right.

Lovable's own published examples: "make the button grey" 0.5 credits, "remove the footer" 0.9, "add authentication with sign-up and login" 1.2, "build me a landing page with images" 1.7–2.0. On those numbers, 100 credits a month sounds generous.

It isn't building that costs money. It's fixing.

The first working version of a screen always costs less than you expect. Every revision after it costs more. Documented cases: a single authentication bug consumed 15 credits — 15% of a Pro month — for something a person would fix by hand in two minutes. Another developer spent an entire month's Pro allowance in one afternoon debugging a Stripe integration. Roughly two thirds of builders report hitting loops where the same fix is attempted three or four different ways.

Auth and payments are two of the three hard things on your build list. That is not a coincidence — they're hard for the same reason they're hard for a person: the failure is invisible in the preview.

The setup order — this part is not optional

If you use Lovable, do these five things before you type a single word about a login or a table. After that point, undoing them costs you real damage.

  1. Create your Supabase project first, in the EU (London) region

    Region is fixed at creation and cannot be changed later. You're holding UK client identity documents and financial records; London is the answer. Do this in the Supabase dashboard, in your own account, before you open Lovable.

  2. Connect GitHub before the first prompt

    Two-way sync: what Lovable writes appears in your repo, what you push to the active branch appears in Lovable. One caveat — it watches one branch, and if you ever disconnect and reconnect it creates a brand new repository and orphans the old one. Connect once, never disconnect.

  3. Connect your own Supabase project — before you ask for anything with a login

    This is the whole ballgame. Ask for auth or a database first and Lovable silently enables Lovable Cloud: a Supabase instance inside their account that you cannot see in any Supabase dashboard, with no service-role key and no database URL, billed through credits. There is no migration path off it.

  4. Turn off model training

    Lovable's terms give them a perpetual licence to use your project data, including for training. The opt-out is free on every plan and applies going forward. Flip it on day one and note the date — you're building a system that will hold client financial records.

  5. One small feature per prompt

    Vague, multi-part prompts are where credits die. "Add a receipt upload button to the receipts page that opens the camera" is a prompt. "Build the receipts feature" is a bill.

Getting out, if you ever want to

Assuming you followed the order above, leaving Lovable is genuinely easy — roughly an hour:

# the repo is already yours; it's a normal Vite app
git clone git@github.com:you/klyara-portal.git
cd klyara-portal
npm install
npm run dev          # it just runs

VITE_SUPABASE_URL=https://xxxx.supabase.co

Your database, storage, users and Edge Functions never moved — they were always in your Supabase account. You lose the visual editor and the preview URL. Nothing else.

If you were on Lovable Cloud instead

Then leaving means: every user's password is lost (force a reset for all of them), every user ID changes to a new UUID so you must hand-patch every table that references one, storage files are downloaded and re-uploaded bucket by bucket, login providers and email templates are rebuilt from scratch, and any table created through the interface rather than a migration file quietly doesn't come with you. Community guides for this run to 33 steps. The existence of four separate third-party migration tools tells you how often people need it.

Chapter five · The recommendation

Build it here instead.

Identical stack, identical output, identical five-day plan — written in this session against the repo, with no meter running and nothing between you and the code.

Why this is the call for you specifically

The four reasons

  • Zero marginal cost. You already pay for this. Lovable adds $25–100/month, and it's the only line in the whole build with an unpredictable meter.
  • No lock-in at all. Your stated position, and the repo is the product from minute one — no Cloud instance, no perpetual data licence, no export step.
  • The design is already decided. Colours, fonts, radii and tone are extracted from the live klyara.com and written into the prompt file. There is no visual exploration to do, which is the one thing Lovable is genuinely better at.
  • The hard parts are the metered parts. RLS policies, the payment webhook, the Bacs state machine. Those need reading and testing, not re-prompting.

What you give up — honestly

  • Click-an-element-and-restyle. Real loss. You describe changes instead of clicking them. For a design system this fixed, it costs you very little.
  • A one-click shareable preview URL. Cloudflare Pages gives you a live preview URL per branch automatically, so this is basically solved — but it's a setup step rather than a default.
  • Automatic back-end provisioning. You create the Supabase project yourself. Ten minutes, once.
  • Handing the keyboard to a non-technical person. Craig can't prompt this. He wasn't going to build the portal anyway.

The build, step by step

  1. Supabase project — EU (London)

    supabase.com → New project → region West EU (London). Copy the project URL and the anon key. Set a real database password and put it in Bitwarden. Ten minutes.

  2. Scaffold the repo

    One command block, then a GitHub repo. This is the only "typing" you personally do.

    npm create vite@latest klyara-portal -- --template react-ts
cd klyara-portal && npm install
npm install @supabase/supabase-js react-router-dom
npm install -D tailwindcss @tailwindcss/vite vite-plugin-pwa
npx shadcn@latest init
git init && git add -A && git commit -m "scaffold"
gh repo create klyara-portal --private --source=. --push
  3. Point me at it and paste the prompts you already have

    The prompt sequence in the build plan works unchanged — Prompt 1 foundations, then signup wizard, receipt upload, admin list, review queue, the email Edge Function, documents, add-on store, notifications, custom domain. Same milestones, same days. The difference is I write to files in the repo and you read the diff.

  4. Database changes as SQL files, in git

    Every table and every RLS policy lands in supabase/migrations/ as a numbered SQL file you can read, review and roll back. Applied with supabase db push. This is the part that protects you.

  5. The Edge Function for receipt routing

    supabase functions deploy route-receipt. Brevo SMTP credentials go in supabase secrets set, never in the repo.

  6. Cloudflare Pages, connected to the repo

    Build command npm run build, output directory dist, two environment variables. Add app.klyara.com as a custom domain — because Cloudflare already runs the DNS for klyara.com it creates the record and the certificate itself, the same way the main site works today. Every push to main deploys automatically; every branch gets its own preview URL. Free. Netlify does the same job identically if you ever prefer it, but there is no reason to add a second dashboard.

  7. Make it installable

    vite-plugin-pwa with the manifest from the prompt file — name Klyara, theme colour #16352A, background #F4EFE4, the K monogram at 192 and 512 px. Half a day including testing on a real phone.

  8. Prove the security before a single real client

    Two test client accounts. Log in as A, try to read B's receipts by ID, confirm you get nothing. Check the built JavaScript bundle for the string service_role — if it's there, stop everything. This is the acceptance test that matters more than any feature.

The door stays open in both directions

Because Lovable syncs two-way with a GitHub repo, you can point it at this repo later if you decide you want the visual editor for a polish pass. You are not choosing permanently — you're choosing what runs the meter this month.

Chapter seven · Getting paid

Direct Debit for retainers. Cards for extras.

Two systems, two jobs. Getting this wrong is the difference between about £600 and about £2,000 a year in fees at thirty clients.

Why Direct Debit, not cards

Note the card row is the commercial card rate, not the 1.5% consumer rate everyone quotes. Your clients are businesses, so that's the rate you'll actually pay on most transactions.

Cost isn't even the main argument. The real ones: you pull the money on the due date instead of chasing an invoice; business cards expire, get reissued and hit limits while a bank mandate just keeps working; and GoCardless integrates natively with Xero, QuickBooks, FreeAgent and Sage, so the collection reconciles itself against the invoice. For a bookkeeper, that reconciliation saving is worth more than the fee difference.

The version you should ship first: no payment code at all

At ten to fifty clients, raise the invoice in QuickBooks and let its built-in GoCardless integration collect it. Zero lines of code. The portal's job is then just to display payment status. Build in-portal checkout only when the add-on store actually needs it — which is the manual's week 6–12 anyway.

When you do wire it in

  1. Never let card or bank details touch your code

    Redirect to Stripe Checkout or the GoCardless hosted page. They render the Direct Debit Guarantee wording and validate the bank details. You handle none of it, and your compliance surface stays tiny.

  2. Three Edge Functions, that's the whole integration

    One creates a checkout session, one returns a customer-portal link so clients change their own bank details, one receives webhooks. About two to four days including testing.

  3. The webhook function is the one that needs care

    Verify the signature. Store every event ID with a unique constraint so a retried webhook can't double-apply. Compare timestamps before overwriting status, because events arrive out of order. Write with the service-role key, and give it no Supabase JWT check — the payment provider doesn't have one.

  4. The payment provider is the source of truth; your database holds a copy

    A subscriptions table mirroring status and period end, an invoices table, and a processed_events table. Gate portal access on the mirrored status, never on a flag you set optimistically when someone finished checkout.

  5. Design for the Bacs delay

    First collection takes four to seven working days to confirm. Don't gate a new client's access on a successful payment — gate it on an active mandate, and suspend later if collections fail.

The compliance bits, briefly

  • No FCA authorisation needed to collect Direct Debits through GoCardless or Stripe — they're the regulated institution, you're a merchant. This changes completely if the portal ever holds or moves money on a client's behalf. Don't build that without advice.
  • AML supervision is separate and is already your critical path. Providing bookkeeping services is regulated under the Money Laundering Regulations — Craig's ICB/AAT route, or HMRC registration at £300 to apply plus £400 per premises a year. Nothing to do with payments; everything to do with whether you can take a client at all.
  • The portal becomes where ID documents live. That's UK GDPR: ICO registration, Supabase's data processing agreement signed, London region, and a retention and deletion schedule built in from day one rather than bolted on.
  • You must give advance notice of the amount and date before each collection. Both providers send these automatically — but confirm the notice period your provider operates under before you promise a client a collection date.

Chapter eight · The native question

Three rungs, and you only need the first.

You were right that this is a web app first. Here's exactly what the other two rungs cost, and the honest reason to climb each one.

RungCostEffortRiskWhen
1 · Installable web app£01–3 daysNone Now. Reaches 100% of clients today, on both platforms.
2 · Google Play$25 once~1 day + listingLow When "we're on the Play Store" is worth something in a sales conversation. It's cheap.
3 · Apple App Store$99/yr + a Mac1–3 weeks, then foreverReal rejection risk Only if an iPhone client's push notifications are demonstrably losing you business.

Rung 1 — what you get for free

Android — effectively a native app

  • You can show your own "Install Klyara" button; Chrome builds a real Android package behind it.
  • Push notifications work without installing — straight from the browser tab.
  • Background sync: a receipt taken with no signal genuinely uploads later.
  • Camera, file access, share-to-Klyara from the gallery. All of it.

iPhone — three real limits

  • Install is manual. Share sheet → Add to Home Screen. No prompt you can trigger. Build the coaching screen — a screenshot with an arrow.
  • Push only after installing. No home-screen icon, no notifications. With ten to fifty clients you walk each one through it during onboarding.
  • No background upload. Queued receipts flush when the app is next open, so say so in the interface rather than pretending.
  • Use <input capture>, not a live camera stream — live video is unreliable in installed iPhone web apps and re-asks permission every launch.

Rung 2 — Google Play, via a Trusted Web Activity

Google's own sanctioned route: your existing site, wrapped, shipped to Play. It shares Chrome's storage, so the login session carries over. PWABuilder or Bubblewrap generates the package.

  • You host one file at /.well-known/assetlinks.json proving you own the domain. The fingerprint in it must be Google's Play signing certificate, not your local upload key — this is the step everyone gets wrong, and the failure mode is a visible browser address bar inside your app.
  • Register as an organisation, not a person. Personal accounts created since late 2023 must run a closed test with twelve testers for fourteen straight days before they can publish. Organisation accounts skip that entirely — but need a D&B D-U-N-S number, which is free and takes days to weeks. Start that early if you want this.
  • Google raises the required Android version every year, so budget half a day annually to rebuild and resubmit.

Rung 3 — Apple, and why I'd defer it

Apple's guideline 4.2 rejects apps that are "a repackaged website". A wrapper whose only job is to load app.klyara.com is the textbook case, and 4.2 is one of the most common rejection reasons there is.

To pass, a Capacitor build has to earn its place: bundle the web assets inside the app rather than pointing at your live URL, add a native tab bar rather than reusing the web menu, add real Apple push notifications, use the native camera plugin for receipts, add Face ID app lock, and ship a proper offline screen. Realistically one to three weeks the first time — most of it Apple push and rebuilding Supabase login for deep links — plus a review cycle on every future fix.

Two smaller traps: keep all pricing and payment out of the iOS app entirely, and supply App Review with working demo credentials or you get auto-rejected at the login screen before anyone reads your submission.

The upside of deferring

If you eventually build the Capacitor version, it produces the Android app from the same codebase — so the Play Store wrapper you'd retire was never wasted work, and none of this requires rewriting the portal. The web app you build in week one is the app in all three cases.
