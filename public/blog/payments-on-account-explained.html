Payments on account: why your January bill is bigger than your tax

27 Jul 2026 · 11 min read · by the Klyara team

30 second versionBreak it down for me

  • Your first self-employed January bill is roughly 150 percent of the tax you expected.
  • You pay the year just ended, plus the first instalment toward the year ahead, on the same date.
  • The second instalment lands 31 July. Both are half of last year's bill.
  • You can apply to reduce them, but under-reduce and HMRC charges interest from the original due dates.

There is a particular January that every self-employed tradesperson remembers: the one where the tax bill was not the number they had saved for. Nothing went wrong and nobody made a mistake. They simply met payments on account for the first time, and nobody had explained them.

First January150%of the bill: the year owed plus half of next
Second payment31 Julythe other half, on account
Trigger£1,000bills above this generally fall into the system

What is actually happening

HMRC's logic is simple: if you owed tax last year, you will probably owe a similar amount this year, so they collect it in advance in two instalments rather than one lump. Each instalment is half of last year's bill. The wrinkle is the changeover year, when you pay the whole of the year just gone plus the first advance instalment for the year ahead, all on the same date.

The gap between the last two bars is the entire problem. Nothing is wrong; the timing is simply not what people expect.

The reducing-payments trap

You can apply to reduce your payments on account if you genuinely expect to earn less. It is a legitimate tool and quiet years are exactly what it exists for. But reduce them too far and HMRC charges interest on the shortfall from the original due dates, which turns a cash-flow tactic into an avoidable cost. Reduce on evidence, from a forecast, not on optimism.

How to make January boring

  • Put a fixed percentage of every payment received into a separate tax account, standing order, same day
  • Use last year's bill as the floor for what to save, not the ceiling
  • Know your two dates: 31 January and 31 July, both diarised, both funded
  • Get your figures done early: knowing the bill in October funds it calmly by January
  • If income has genuinely dropped, apply to reduce with a forecast behind it, not a hope

The habit that solves it permanently

Live books mean the tax figure is known long before it is due. Every Klyara client sees the building liability in their portal all year, which turns the January bill from an event into a line item.

The full timeline of a first self-employed tax year

  • 6 April
    Year one starts

    You begin trading. No tax is due yet, and it feels free.

  • 5 April
    Year one ends

    Your first full tax year is complete. The bill exists now, even though nothing has been asked for.

  • Following 31 January
    The big one

    The whole of year one's tax, plus the first payment on account for year two, due together.

  • 31 July
    Second instalment

    The other half of year two's estimate.

  • Next 31 January
    It settles down

    Any balance for year two, plus the first instalment for year three. From here it is a rhythm.

The pain is concentrated entirely in that first January, because two years of liability meet on one date.

How much to actually set aside

The honest rule of thumb for a sole trader with no other income is somewhere between 25 and 35 percent of profit, once income tax and Class 4 National Insurance are both counted, and higher once you cross into the higher-rate band. Percentage of turnover is a dangerous shortcut for anyone with heavy materials costs, because turnover and profit can be wildly different numbers on the same job.

ProfitRough tax and NISet aside

Indicative only, for a sole trader with no other income, using current rates and allowances. Your own position will differ; the point is the ballpark and the habit.

The budget payment plan nobody uses

HMRC lets you pay by regular direct debit toward a future bill through a budget payment plan. It costs nothing, it is not a loan, and it turns January from an event into a standing order. It is the single most underused feature in the entire Self Assessment system.

Where CIS deductions fit

If you are a CIS subcontractor, the 20 percent already withheld counts toward this bill and is often enough to cover it entirely, which is why so many subbies receive refunds rather than demands. But the deductions come off labour only, so a subbie with high labour and low materials is usually well covered, while one invoicing heavy materials can still face a bill despite having had money withheld all year. That is exactly the gap our quarterly figures are designed to show you in October rather than January.

January goes smoothly when

  • The bill was known by October
  • A separate tax account has been funded all year
  • Payments on account are expected, not discovered
  • Any reduction claim is backed by a forecast

January hurts when

  • The return is filed in the last week
  • Tax money was spent as working capital
  • The 150 percent is a surprise
  • A reduction was claimed on optimism and interest follows
