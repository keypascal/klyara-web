The jargon, translated

Construction money,
in plain English.

Twenty-six terms that decide whether you get paid properly and taxed correctly, each explained the way we would on the phone: short, straight, and with the trap named.

A formal 'this is what we are owed so far' request on bigger contracts, replacing simple invoices. Different paperwork rhythm, different chasing rules: get it wrong and payment cycles slip a month. The rhythm matters as much as the paperwork: applications go in on the contract's dates, certificates come back, and payment follows the certified figure, not your hopes. Miss a date and you can legally wait a whole extra cycle.
Usually 3 to 5 percent held back from your payments: half released at practical completion, half a year later. Diarise both or donate them to the client, which the industry does at scandalous scale. Track them like invoices with long fuses: value, release trigger, and the person who signs it off. Our books diarise both halves the day the contract starts, because 'we forgot' is the industry's most expensive sentence.
The formal moment the works are agreed done bar snagging. Starts the defects period and releases the first half of retention: a date worth celebrating and diarising. Get it certified in writing, dated: it starts clocks on retention, defects liability and final accounts. A vague 'yeah, we're done' costs real money when the second retention half needs a trigger date.
The QS's assessment of work done to date on a contract, which drives what your application gets certified at. Photograph everything; valuations respect evidence. Dated photos, delivery notes and signed day-work sheets turn a QS's opinion into your number. The habit of evidencing as you build is worth points of margin on every contested valuation.
8 percent above base plus a fixed fee (40 to 100 pounds) that the law adds to late B2B invoices, whether or not the contract mentions it. Our late-payment tool computes it; our chasing letters cite it. It applies automatically to B2B debts: no contract clause needed, no permission required. Quoting the accrued figure in a reminder changes payer behaviour faster than any tone of voice we have tested.
The days a customer has to pay. Unstated terms default to 30 days B2B: state yours on every quote and invoice, or inherit the default and the arguments. Put them on the quote, the order confirmation AND the invoice: courts side with the paper trail. Fourteen days is normal in domestic work; thirty creeps in commercially: choose deliberately rather than inherit.
The Construction Industry Scheme: contractors deduct tax from subcontractors' labour before paying them and hand it to HMRC. The whole scheme in one sentence; the details fill our guide. It is not optional and it is not income tax done: deductions are advance payments credited at year end, which is why refunds exist. Contractors carry the compliance duty; subbies carry the cashflow hit.
The monthly slip a contractor must give each subbie showing what was held back. Your refund evidence: chase missing ones like unpaid invoices, because they are. By law it must arrive within 14 days of each tax month end. No statement, no easy refund evidence: chase in writing, keep copies, and HMRC's own records break ties when statements go missing.
The CIS grade where contractors pay you with zero deductions: cashflow transforms overnight. Earned via turnover and compliance tests; protected by never filing late. HMRC reviews compliance annually and revokes for lateness: one sloppy year can undo it. Firms with gross status guard their filing calendar like a trade secret, because it effectively is one.
Registered subbies lose 20 percent of labour to CIS; unregistered lose 30. Registration takes minutes and is the fastest pay rise in construction. The 10-point gap is pure registration paperwork: on 40,000 pounds of labour it is 4,000 pounds of cashflow a year. If you know an unregistered subbie, send them the link; it is the easiest favour in the trade.
CIS is deducted from labour only, never materials: itemise them separately on every invoice or watch tax be deducted from your timber. Keep supplier invoices matched to each job: HMRC can ask a contractor to evidence the split, and unevidenced materials get reclassified as labour: deducted, painfully, in arrears.
What HMRC owes a subbie whose deductions beat their tax bill: the average cheque runs to four figures. Filed early (from 6 April), it lands in weeks. Limited companies offset through PAYE instead: different mechanics, same money. Either way the speed lever is filing early with clean statements: April filers see money while July filers are still finding paperwork.
Making Tax Digital for Income Tax: from April 2026 (50k+ gross), sole traders keep digital records and send quarterly updates plus a final declaration. The reason half this site exists. The thresholds step down: 50k gross from April 2026, 30k from 2027, 20k from 2028, measured on gross qualifying income before CIS deductions. Most subbies over the line do not feel over the line: that is the trap.
The MTD summary of income and expenses sent each quarter (due 7 Aug, 7 Nov, 7 Feb, 7 May). Not a tax bill: a digital postcard proving the records are live. It is cumulative and correctable: an error in Q1 washes through by Q4, so updates are low-stakes if the records behind them are real. The panic belongs to shoebox systems, not digital ones.
MTD's year-end step replacing the Self Assessment return: due 31 January with the balancing payment. The quarters make it boring, which is the point. Everything the quarters reported gets finalised, adjustments added (capital allowances, other income), and the bill confirmed. With live books it is a reading exercise: the number is already known by October.
Advance instalments toward next year's tax, due 31 January and 31 July: the classic first-year ambush. Forecast them and January stops biting. Each instalment is half of last year's bill. Profits falling? You can apply to reduce them, but under-reduce and interest applies. The honest fix is a rolling forecast, which the books provide free.
VAT rule for construction B2B: the customer accounts for the VAT, your invoice shows none. Wrecks cashflow assumptions and confuses software defaults; ours is configured for it on day one. End-users and intermediary suppliers are excluded: the words 'end user statement' on file decide whose VAT rules apply. Software must be told per-contact: ours is, which is why our invoices survive inspections.
Real Time Information: payroll must be reported to HMRC on or before every payday, not monthly in arrears. Why DIY Friday payroll is a compliance sport. Late FPS filings attract per-month penalties once HMRC's patience lapses, and 'the van broke down' is not a filing exception. Approval-from-the-cab exists precisely because paydays do not move.
90,000 pounds of taxable turnover in ANY rolling 12 months triggers registration: watch the rolling number, not the tax year. Our tracker tool does exactly that. There is also a deregistration threshold (88k) and a 30-day forward-look test: expect to breach in the next month alone and registration is due immediately. The rolling test catches the past; the forward test catches the big contract.
MTD lateness earns points; four points converts to a 200 pound fine and each further miss fines again. A 24-month clean streak wipes the slate: recovery is designed in. Points expire individually after 24 compliant months, and each obligation carries its own tally. The design rewards getting back on the horse: one bad patch is recoverable by simply being boring afterwards.
Legal shapes for the firm: simplicity and one tax return, versus liability protection and salary-plus-dividends. The right answer changes with profit: rerun it yearly, not never. The crossover is profit-dependent and moves with dividend tax rates: the rule of thumb changes every Budget. Rerun the sums at year end with real numbers: our partner accountant does exactly this for clients.
Employees taxed at source through payroll software with RTI filings each run: distinct from CIS subbies in law, paperwork and pension duties. Mixing the two up is a five-figure tribunal hobby. Status is decided by working reality, not the label on the invoice: control, substitution, equipment. Get a regular 'subbie' wrong and back-tax, NI and pension duties arrive together, with interest.
The duty to assess staff and enrol eligible ones into a pension from their first payday, with re-enrolment every three years. Fully carried inside our payroll rails. Postponement can defer assessment three months for new starters: useful for short-term crew, dangerous if you forget the diary date. Re-enrolment every three years catches the opt-outs again, by design.
Bookkeeping is the living monthly record (us); accountancy is year-end accounts and tax strategy (our partner, or yours). Great year-ends are built on great books: that order, always. Paying year-end rates for data entry is the classic small-firm money leak: the accountant rebuilding a shoebox bills archaeology at advisory prices. Split the roles and both bills shrink.
Money in and out mapped forward 13 weeks: the difference between knowing a crunch is coming and meeting it in the dark. Lives in the portal, updated by the books themselves. Thirteen weeks is the horizon that catches VAT quarters, CIS cycles and payroll in one view. The firms that survive bad quarters are rarely the most profitable: they are the ones that saw the dip eight weeks out.
The costs that arrive whether or not you are on the tools: van, insurance, phone, software, the yard. The number most under-priced day rates forgot: our rate builder refuses to. Divide the annual total by billable days and put that number in every quote as a line you refuse to discount below. Firms that price from 'what others charge' are pricing from other people's overheads.
