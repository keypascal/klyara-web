Getting paid

The 13-week cash forecast every building firm should run

24 Aug 2026 · 12 min read · by the Klyara team

30 second versionBreak it down for me

  • Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact. Construction pays late by design, so the two disagree constantly.
  • Thirteen weeks is the right horizon: it covers a VAT quarter, three CIS returns and thirteen payrolls.
  • Forecast by the week money actually lands, not the invoice date. Use each customer's real payment behaviour.
  • A red week spotted eight weeks out has cheap fixes. Spotted eight days out, every option costs money.

Profit and cash are not the same thing, and construction is the sector where that gap does the most damage. You can finish a quarter with a healthy profit on paper and still be unable to pay a supplier on Friday, because the profit is sitting in an application for payment that gets certified next month and paid the month after. Firms rarely fail because the work was bad. They fail because money left faster than it arrived, and nobody saw it coming until the bank said no.

Why thirteen weeks

Thirteen weeks is one quarter, and a quarter is the natural rhythm of a building firm's obligations: a VAT return, three CIS returns, thirteen weekly payrolls or three monthly ones, and usually one big supplier account settling up. Look four weeks ahead and you only see the fire you are already in. Look twelve months ahead and the numbers are fiction. Thirteen weeks is the window where your figures are still real but the trouble is still avoidable.

Two thirds of the money earned is somewhere other than your bank. That is normal, and it is exactly why a forecast beats a bank balance.

Building it: three columns, nothing clever

  1. 1
    Money in, by the week it will actually land

    Not the invoice date: the payment date, based on what that customer has historically done. A 30-day customer who always pays in 47 goes in at week seven, not week four.

  2. 2
    Money out, in the order it leaves

    Wages first, because they never slip. Then subbies, suppliers, HMRC, finance, rent, and the standing order pile most firms forget to total.

  3. 3
    Running balance, week by week

    Opening balance plus in, minus out. The moment a week goes red is the moment you make a decision, eight weeks before your bank does.

The one that catches everyone

VAT and CIS are not your money, but they sit in your account looking exactly like it is. A forecast that ignores the quarterly VAT payment and the monthly CIS liability will tell you that you are fine right up until the day you are not.

What to do when a week goes red

The value of seeing it early is that cheap fixes still work. Eight weeks out you can chase an old invoice, ask a good customer to settle early for a small discount, move a non-urgent equipment purchase back a month, or agree a payment plan with HMRC before the debt exists. Eight days out, your only options are expensive: an overdraft extension, invoice finance at short notice, or paying wages late, which costs you crew you will not easily replace.

The weekly twenty minutes

  • Update what actually landed against what you forecast, and note who paid late again
  • Add any new certified applications and invoices with realistic payment dates
  • Check the next four weeks for red, and act on the earliest one
  • Confirm the tax lines: VAT quarter, CIS by the 19th, payroll and pension dates
  • Note one thing that surprised you, because that is where your terms need work

Every Klyara client on Trade and above gets this forecast maintained from their live books, not rebuilt from memory each month. The figures come out of the same records the returns are filed from, so the forecast and the tax position never disagree. See it running on your numbers.

The four numbers that drive every forecast

Most forecasts fail because they are built from wishes. These four inputs, taken from your own history rather than your hopes, are what make the difference between a spreadsheet and a warning system.

Pull all four from the books rather than from memory and the forecast stops being fiction within one cycle.

Reading the shape, not just the numbers

A forecast is a picture, and three shapes come up constantly in construction. The sawtooth is healthy: money dips as you fund a stage, then recovers when it is certified. The staircase down means every cycle ends lower than the last, which is a pricing or a payment-terms problem, not a cash problem, and no amount of chasing will fix it. The cliff is a single large outflow, usually a VAT quarter or a tax payment on account, that the running balance cannot absorb. Each shape has a different remedy, which is why looking at the line matters more than staring at the closing balance.

Signs the forecast is working

  • You know your worst week before it arrives
  • Chasing starts before an invoice is old
  • Tax dates are funded, not feared
  • You can answer 'can we take this job' with a number

Signs you are flying blind

  • The bank balance is your only measure
  • VAT quarters arrive as a shock
  • You find out about a squeeze from a supplier
  • Big contracts get accepted on gut feel

What to do with each red week

  1. 1
    Eight or more weeks out: negotiate

    Ring the customer with the oldest balance, offer a small settlement discount, move a discretionary purchase back a month. Everything here is free or nearly free.

  2. 2
    Four to eight weeks out: restructure

    Agree a time-to-pay arrangement with HMRC before the debt exists, stage an upcoming job more aggressively, or bring a deposit forward on work already agreed.

  3. 3
    Under four weeks: finance

    Invoice finance, an overdraft extension, or a director loan. All of these cost money, which is precisely why seeing the problem early is worth so much.

  4. 4
    Never: pay wages late

    Crew leave and do not come back. Every other option, including uncomfortable conversations with suppliers, is cheaper than rebuilding a team.

The firms that survive a bad quarter are rarely the most profitable ones. They are the ones who saw the dip eight weeks out and had time to do something cheap about it.

What thirteen weeks of visibility actually buys

Time-to-pay: the option most firms ignore

HMRC's Time to Pay service exists precisely for businesses whose profits are real but whose timing is not. Approached before a deadline with a forecast in hand, arrangements are routinely agreed and interest is charged at a rate far below emergency commercial borrowing. Approached after a missed deadline with no plan, the same conversation is harder and penalties are already running. The forecast is what turns that call from an admission into a proposal.

Set it up once, keep it forever

  • A separate tax account that VAT and CIS money moves into on receipt, automatically
  • Payment terms stated on quote, order and invoice, and chasing that starts on day one overdue
  • Debtor days tracked per customer, so you know who to stop working for
  • Retention release dates diarised at contract signature, not at the end
  • The forecast reviewed the same twenty minutes every week, in the same seat, forever
