Hire or buy? The plant decision, run on real numbers

6 Jul 2026 · 10 min read · by the Klyara team

30 second versionBreak it down for me

  • Utilisation decides it: how many days a year the machine actually earns.
  • Below roughly a hundred days a year, hire almost always wins.
  • Count the forgotten ownership costs: insurance, servicing, transport, storage, downtime, finance.
  • Own what you use weekly, hire the specialist machine for each job.

Owning plant feels like progress. Hiring feels like money leaving. The honest answer is decided by one number that most firms never calculate: how many days a year you actually use the thing.

The deciding numberUtilisationdays used per year, honestly counted
Rough crossover~90-120days a year for common items
Forgotten costs5-8insurance, servicing, transport, storage, downtime, finance

The calculation, done properly

  1. 1
    Count real usage days from last year

    Not the days you had it. The days it earned. Most firms overestimate by half, which is the entire reason bad purchases happen.

  2. 2
    Total the true annual cost of owning

    Finance or capital cost spread over its life, insurance, servicing, tyres and tracks, transport between sites, storage, and the days it sits broken.

  3. 3
    Compare against the hire cost for those same days

    Include delivery and collection each time, because that is where hire quietly gets expensive on short jobs.

  4. 4
    Then add the flexibility premium

    Hire lets you take the right machine for each job. Ownership means using the machine you have, whether or not it suits.

The middle road most firms miss

Own the items you use weekly and hire the ones you use occasionally. A firm that owns its everyday kit and hires the specialist machine for each job typically beats both the buy-everything and hire-everything approaches.

The tax angle, briefly

Hire charges are an ordinary deductible expense in the period they arise. Purchased plant usually qualifies for the Annual Investment Allowance, giving full relief in the year of purchase, with a balancing adjustment when you sell. Neither is automatically better; the tax follows the commercial decision rather than driving it. See how capital allowances work.

The utilisation lie every firm tells itself

Ask a firm how often they use the machine and they will say 'all the time'. Ask them to count the days from job records and the answer is routinely half. Count first, buy second.

The middle options people forget

  1. 1
    Long-term hire

    Weeks or months at a discounted rate. Fixed cost, no maintenance risk, no capital tied up.

  2. 2
    Contract hire with maintenance

    Effectively an operating lease. Predictable monthly cost, and the supplier carries downtime risk.

  3. 3
    Buy used, sell on

    Depreciation on used plant is far gentler than new, and the residual is more predictable.

  4. 4
    Share with another local firm

    Common, informal, and workable with a written agreement on maintenance, insurance and priority.

  5. 5
    Hire in the operator too

    On specialist plant this often costs less than training and certifying your own crew.

Whichever way you go, cost it to the job rather than to the firm. Plant charged into job costing shows you which work actually justifies the machine. See how job costing works.
