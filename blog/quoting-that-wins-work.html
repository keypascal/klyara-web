Winning work

Quotes that win work without cutting your price

13 Jul 2026 · 12 min read · by the Klyara team

30 second versionBreak it down for me

  • Most quotes lose on presentation and timing, not price.
  • Quote within 24 hours, break the price into blocks, and show two or three photos of similar jobs.
  • State exclusions explicitly and say in writing how changes get priced.
  • Follow up at day three and day ten. Most quotes are never chased once, and that is where the work goes.

Two firms quote the same extension. One sends a text saying "18k all in, can start March". The other sends a one-page document with a breakdown, three dated photos of similar work, a start window, and a line explaining what happens if the customer changes their mind halfway. The second firm quotes 19,500 and wins more often. Nothing about the work is different. Everything about the confidence is.

What a homeowner is actually buying

They are not buying joinery. They are buying certainty that a stranger will turn up, do what they said, not vanish mid-job, and not surprise them with the bill. Every element of a good quote reduces one of those fears. Price is what they discuss because it is the only thing they know how to compare; certainty is what actually decides it.

  1. 1
    Send it within 24 hours

    Speed reads as competence and as wanting the work. The firm that quotes first is remembered as the professional one.

  2. 2
    Break the price into three or four blocks

    Not a full costing: labour, materials, and any specialist elements. A single number invites haggling; a structure invites questions you can answer.

  3. 3
    Show two or three similar jobs

    Dated photos of your own work beat any adjective. This is where the trade wins against national firms.

  4. 4
    State exactly what is included, and what is not

    Skips, making good, decorating, scaffolding, parking. Ambiguity is where arguments and margin leaks live.

  5. 5
    Say what happens if things change

    One line on variations turns the awkward mid-job conversation into something you already agreed in writing.

  6. 6
    End with a clear next step and a date

    'Happy to hold the March slot until the 12th' beats 'let me know'.

The line that raises prices

'This quote holds for 30 days and includes everything listed. If you want anything changed, we will price it in writing before doing it.' It reads as professional, it protects your margin, and it quietly signals that you are not a firm that gets pushed around.

Following up is not pestering

Most quotes are never followed up once. A short message at day three and again at day ten wins work that would otherwise go to whoever happened to ring. The customer is usually not rejecting you; they are busy, and the first firm to be politely present when they finally think about it gets the job.

Every enquiry that gets quoted has to be captured first, which is why the phone matters as much as the paperwork. See how every call gets answered.

The anatomy of a quote that converts

  1. 1
    A short cover paragraph in your own words

    Two sentences: what you understood they want, and that you have done this before. This is where trust starts, and almost nobody bothers.

  2. 2
    The price, broken into blocks

    Labour, materials, specialist works, and any provisional sums. Enough structure to answer questions, not so much that you are handing over your costings.

  3. 3
    Inclusions and exclusions, side by side

    The clearest section in the document. Every dispute you avoid was written down here.

  4. 4
    Programme

    A start window, a duration, and the honest list of what can move it.

  5. 5
    Proof

    Two or three dated photos of similar work, and a line about how long you have been trading locally.

  6. 6
    Terms and next step

    Payment schedule, validity period, and one clear action with a date attached.

A quote is a sales document, not a spreadsheet

Customers cannot judge your joinery. They judge your paperwork, your punctuality and your clarity, because those are the only things they can assess before committing. Everything about a professional quote is a proxy for how the job will run.

Pricing psychology that is not a trick

Three genuine, honest effects worth knowing. First, an itemised price is easier to accept than a single lump, because the customer can see where the money goes. Second, offering a good-better-best structure on suitable jobs (standard fittings, mid-range, premium) moves the conversation from whether to how much, and most people choose the middle. Third, the first number a customer sees anchors everything afterwards, which is why a firm that quotes properly and confidently rarely ends up as the cheap option, and rarely needs to be.
