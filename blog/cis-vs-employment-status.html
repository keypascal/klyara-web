CIS

Is your subbie really self-employed? The status test that lands firms in trouble

15 Jun 2026 · 12 min read · by the Klyara team

30 second versionBreak it down for me

  • HMRC decides employment status on working reality, not on what the invoice says.
  • Control, substitution and financial risk are the three questions that settle it.
  • The bill for getting it wrong lands on the contractor, and can look back up to six years.
  • Run HMRC's status tool, save the result with the date, and keep a short written agreement.

A subbie who works only for you, uses your tools, starts when you say and cannot send someone in their place is not a subcontractor because the invoice says so. They are, in employment-law and tax terms, quite likely an employee. HMRC decides on the working reality, not the paperwork, and when they decide differently to you, the bill lands on the contractor.

The three questions that decide it

  1. 1
    Control: who decides how the work is done?

    Being told what to achieve is normal. Being told how, when to start, when to break and in what order points hard at employment.

  2. 2
    Substitution: can they send someone else?

    A genuine business can send a competent replacement. If only that person will do, the arrangement looks personal, which looks like employment.

  3. 3
    Financial risk: can they lose money on it?

    Quoting a price, correcting defects at their own cost, providing their own tools and insurance all say business. Paid by the hour with no risk says employee.

Points to self-employmentPoints to employment
Provides main tools and plantUses your tools and van
Quotes per job or per stagePaid hourly with no risk
Works for several contractorsWorks only for you, long term
Can send a substituteMust attend personally
Fixes defects at own costSnags fixed on your time
Holds own insuranceCovered by yours

No single line settles it. HMRC weighs the overall picture, which is why documenting your reasoning matters.

The five-minute protection

Run HMRC's own status tool for every regular subbie, save the result as a PDF with the date, and keep a short written agreement covering substitution and how the work is priced. If the arrangement is ever questioned, contemporaneous evidence is worth far more than a later explanation.

What the fix looks like

Sometimes the honest answer is that the person should be on payroll, and that is not a disaster: it costs employer's National Insurance and pension contributions, most of which the Employment Allowance softens for small firms, and it buys you a stable crew and no exposure. Other times the arrangement is genuinely self-employed and simply needs tidier evidence. Either way, deciding deliberately beats discovering accidentally.

We run both sides in one place: CIS subbies and PAYE crew, in a single monthly rhythm, with the status question flagged before it becomes a problem. See payroll and CIS together.

What HMRC actually looks at, in order

There is no single test and no magic clause. HMRC and the courts weigh the whole picture, but three factors carry most of the weight, and a fourth quietly decides many cases.

FactorPoints to self-employmentPoints to employment
Personal serviceA genuine, usable right of substitutionMust turn up personally, always
ControlYou agree the outcome and they decide howTold what, how, when and in what order
Financial riskQuotes, fixes defects unpaid, provides kitPaid by the hour, no downside risk
Part and parcelComes and goes as a separate businessHas a company email, van, uniform and a place in the team

The fourth row is the one that surprises firms. A long-serving 'subbie' who is treated exactly like staff usually is staff.

Mutuality of obligation, in plain terms

If you are obliged to offer work and they are obliged to accept it, that looks like employment. A genuine subcontractor can turn down a job without consequence, and you can stop offering without notice or process.

Fixing it without a crisis

  1. 1
    Review every long-term arrangement now

    The ones that worry you are the ones you already suspect. Start there.

  2. 2
    Run CEST and keep the output

    Date it, save it, file it with the worker's records. Contemporaneous evidence is worth vastly more than a later explanation.

  3. 3
    Write down the arrangement

    A short agreement covering substitution, how work is priced, whose tools, and who carries defect risk.

  4. 4
    Where the answer is employment, move them properly

    Payroll, pension, holiday. It costs employer's NI and pension, softened by the Employment Allowance for most small firms, and it removes the exposure entirely.

  5. 5
    Take advice on historic exposure before disclosing

    If a past arrangement was clearly wrong, there are structured ways to correct it. Do that with professional help rather than in a panic.

HMRC does not read your invoice. It reads your working week.
