Getting paid

Retentions: tracking the money you have earned but not got

14 Aug 2026 · 10 min read · by the Klyara team

30 second versionBreak it down for me

  • Three to five percent of each valuation, held for months or years after completion.
  • Retention is money you have earned; it belongs in your books as a debtor, not as a surprise.
  • Roughly one pound in five of retention is never collected across the sector.
  • Diarise both release dates at contract signature, and chase them like any other invoice.

Retentions are the construction habit outsiders never believe: the customer keeps 3 to 5% of every valuation, half of it until practical completion, the rest for a defects period that can run a year or more. Money you earned, sitting in someone else's account, on a promise.

Where it goes wrong

Industry surveys have estimated hundreds of millions in retentions go unpaid across the sector every year, through insolvency upstream, through disputes, and most often through nobody ever asking. Release dates arrive silently; no invoice is triggered; the money just quietly never comes home. On a firm doing £500,000 a year at 5%, the pot at risk is £25,000. That is a van.

Run retentions like debts, because they are

Book them. Each retention sits in your accounts as money owed, with its release dates, not as a vague memory. Diary the dates. Practical completion and end of defects both go in the calendar the day the contract is signed. Invoice on the day. Release rarely happens unprompted; the firms that collect are simply the firms that ask. Watch who holds your money. A retention is an unsecured loan to your customer; if their name starts appearing in the wrong conversations, chase harder.

On our books, retentions are tracked as receivables with dates attached, and the chasing service does the asking. Earned money should come home on schedule, every time. See chasing on autopilot.

Tracking it so it does not vanish

  1. 1
    Record retention as a debtor from day one

    It is money earned. If it only exists as a note in your head, it will be forgotten when the contract ends.

  2. 2
    Diarise both release dates at signature

    Half at practical completion, the balance at the end of the defects period, often twelve months later.

  3. 3
    Chase the first half the week it falls due

    Retention is rarely released proactively. Assume you will have to ask.

  4. 4
    Clear snags fast and evidence it

    The defects period is the excuse for withholding the second half. Photograph completed remedials.

  5. 5
    Escalate on the same ladder as any invoice

    Statutory interest applies to overdue retention on commercial contracts too.

Ask before you sign

Retention is negotiable more often than firms assume, particularly on smaller subcontracts. A retention bond, a lower percentage, or a shorter defects period are all things a main contractor may agree to if asked at tender stage rather than after.

Retention handled well

  • Recorded as a debtor per contract
  • Both dates diarised at signature
  • Snags cleared and evidenced
  • Chased like any other invoice

Retention lost

  • Remembered vaguely as 'some money owed'
  • Nobody knows the release date
  • Snag list never formally closed
  • Written off when the job feels finished
