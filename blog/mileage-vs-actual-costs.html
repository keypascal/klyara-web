Mileage or actual costs: which van method leaves you better off

1 Jun 2026 · 10 min read · by the Klyara team

30 second versionBreak it down for me

  • Two methods: 45p per mile for the first 10,000 business miles, or actual running costs plus capital allowances.
  • High mileage in a cheap van favours the flat rate. Low mileage in an expensive van favours actual costs.
  • Once you use the mileage rate for a vehicle, you must keep using it for that vehicle.
  • Both methods need a mileage log. Home to a permanent workplace is commuting and does not count.

Two ways to claim the van, and firms routinely pick the wrong one, then discover they cannot switch back for that vehicle. The choice is worth hundreds a year and takes one calculation to get right.

Simplified rate45pper mile for the first 10,000 business miles, 25p after
Actual costsEverythingfuel, insurance, repairs, tax, plus capital allowances
Crossover~15-20kmiles a year, for a typical working van

The two methods, plainly

Simplified mileage gives you a flat rate per business mile and asks nothing else of you but a mileage log. It covers fuel, servicing, insurance, depreciation, everything. Simple, defensible, and usually generous on a cheap, fuel-efficient vehicle doing high miles.

Actual costs claims the real running expenses, apportioned for private use, plus capital allowances on the vehicle itself. Better for expensive vans, thirsty vehicles, heavy repair years, and anyone doing modest mileage in a vehicle that cost real money.

The rule that surprises people

Once you claim the simplified mileage rate for a particular vehicle, you must keep using it for that vehicle for as long as you own it. Choose deliberately at the start, not casually.

What the records have to show

Either method needs evidence

  • A mileage log: date, journey, purpose, miles. An app doing it automatically is fine and far better than memory
  • Home to a permanent workplace is commuting and does not count. Home to a site does, in most trade patterns
  • Actual-cost claims need every receipt: fuel, servicing, insurance, MOT, tyres, repairs
  • A sensible, consistent private-use percentage you can justify, not a round number picked to be flattering
  • Purchase paperwork for the vehicle if you are claiming capital allowances

Vans, tools and fuel are also where the cars-versus-vans distinction bites: a double-cab pickup is not automatically treated the way people assume. Worth a conversation before you buy, not after. Ask us before you sign for the vehicle.

Working out your own crossover

  1. 1
    Add up a year of real vehicle costs

    Fuel, insurance, road tax, MOT, servicing, tyres, repairs, breakdown cover, and finance interest if applicable.

  2. 2
    Apply your honest business percentage

    If the van does the school run, that is private use and it comes out.

  3. 3
    Add the capital allowance

    On a purchased van, the Annual Investment Allowance can give full relief in year one, which often tips the first year decisively toward actual costs.

  4. 4
    Compare against 45p and 25p

    First 10,000 business miles at the higher rate, everything above at the lower one.

  5. 5
    Choose deliberately, then stay

    For that vehicle, the choice sticks until you replace it.

ScenarioUsually betterWhy
Cheap van, 20,000+ business milesMileage rateFlat rate outruns modest real costs
Expensive van, 8,000 milesActual costsCapital allowances and real depreciation dominate
Van bought outright this yearActual costs, year oneAIA relief is large and immediate
Older paid-off van, high milesMileage rateLittle left to claim; the flat rate is generous
Mixed private and business useDependsApportionment applies to actual costs; mileage is inherently clean

Run both calculations once. It takes twenty minutes and the answer holds for the life of the vehicle.

Cars, vans, and the pickup problem

Vans are plant; cars are restricted. Double-cab pickups have been treated inconsistently and the classification affects both capital allowances and, for company vehicles, benefit-in-kind. Confirm treatment before you sign, not after.

Records that survive a check

The mileage log that keeps you safe

  • Date, start and end point, purpose, and miles for each business journey
  • An app logging automatically beats memory and beats a notebook found in a glovebox
  • Note the odometer at the start and end of the tax year
  • Keep it contemporaneously: reconstructed logs are exactly what HMRC challenges
  • If you claim actual costs, keep every fuel and repair receipt as well

Mileage method

  • No receipts to keep beyond the log
  • Simple, predictable, defensible
  • Generous on fuel-efficient high-mileage vans

Actual costs method

  • Every receipt must be kept and coded
  • Better on expensive or thirsty vehicles
  • Capital allowances available on the vehicle
