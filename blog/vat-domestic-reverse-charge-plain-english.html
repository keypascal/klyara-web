VAT, construction

The VAT domestic reverse charge, in plain English

27 July 2026, 12 min read · by the Klyara team

30 second versionBreak it down for me

  • On most B2B construction between VAT and CIS registered firms, you charge no VAT and the customer accounts for it.
  • End users, meaning customers not selling the work on, get normal VAT, but get their status in writing.
  • Many subbies became permanent reclaimers: monthly VAT returns turn that into monthly cash.
  • Wrong either way costs money. The customer's status decides it, not the job type.

The domestic reverse charge for construction arrived in March 2021, and five years on it still causes more invoice arguments than any rule in the sector. It is not actually complicated; it is just explained badly, usually by people billing by the hour to explain it. Here is the whole thing in one read.

The one-sentence version

For most VAT-registered construction work between businesses in the CIS chain, the supplier no longer charges VAT. The customer accounts for it instead on their own VAT return.

Instead of you adding 20 percent to your invoice, collecting it and passing it to HMRC, your customer does the maths at their end. The money never makes the round trip. HMRC brought this in because too much VAT was being collected by suppliers who then vanished, so they removed the cash from the chain.

When it applies: the checklist

The reverse charge applies when all of these are true:

  • The supply is construction services within the scope of CIS: building, alteration, repair, civils, demolition, M and E installation and the like.
  • Both parties are VAT-registered.
  • Both parties are CIS-registered, with payment reported under CIS.
  • The rate would normally be standard or reduced; zero-rated work is out.
  • Your customer is not an end user. More on that now.

The end-user bit everyone forgets

The reverse charge is for the middle of the chain: subbie to contractor, contractor to main contractor. It stops at the end. If your customer is the end user, meaning they are not selling the construction service on, think the property owner or a business refurbishing its own premises, normal VAT rules apply and you charge VAT as usual.

Crucially, it is on the customer to tell you in writing that they are an end user. No written confirmation and the other conditions met? Reverse charge applies. Get that confirmation into your paperwork habits; it settles most arguments before they start.

Same logic for homeowners and anyone not VAT-registered: domestic extensions, loft conversions, a kitchen for Mrs Patel. Normal VAT, always. The reverse charge never touches consumer work.

How to invoice it, exactly

  1. Show no VAT charged. The total is the net amount.
  2. State that the reverse charge applies, with wording like: "Reverse charge: customer to pay the VAT to HMRC".
  3. Show the rate and the amount the customer must account for, for example "VAT of 2,400 at 20 percent to be accounted for by the customer".

Send an old-style invoice with 20 percent added and one of two bad things happens: a sharp customer bounces it and you wait another payment run, or a sloppy customer pays it and you are sitting on VAT you charged incorrectly, with a correction to unwind. Either way you lose time, cash flow, or both.

The cash-flow sting for subbies

Under the old rules, the VAT you collected sat in your account until the quarterly return, an interest-free float many firms quietly relied on. The reverse charge removed it. One genuine fix: if most of your sales are now reverse-charge, you likely reclaim more input VAT than you owe. Switching to monthly VAT returns turns that into a monthly refund and permanently improves cash flow. It is one of the first things we check when a subbie joins Klyara.

The five mistakes we fix most often

  • Charging VAT to a contractor anyway. The classic. Bounced invoice, delayed payment.
  • Reverse-charging an end user. Undercharging HMRC's money; awkward corrections follow.
  • No written end-user confirmations on file. When status is wrong, the paper trail decides who carries the can.
  • Software half-configured: reverse charge set up on sales but not purchases, so the VAT return quietly disagrees with reality.
  • Mixed supplies bodged: jobs spanning CIS and non-CIS work coded as one line. There are rules for this; hope is not one of them.

The decision tree, in order

  1. 1
    Is the work within CIS and standard or reduced rated?

    Zero-rated new build work is outside the reverse charge because there is no VAT to shift.

  2. 2
    Is your customer VAT registered?

    If not, charge VAT normally. Homeowners always get normal VAT.

  3. 3
    Are they CIS registered?

    Both parties need to be in CIS for the reverse charge to apply.

  4. 4
    Are they an end user or intermediary?

    Someone not selling the construction service on. If they confirm that in writing, charge VAT normally.

  5. 5
    Otherwise, reverse charge applies

    No VAT on your invoice, with wording stating the customer must account for it.

Reverse charge applies

  • Subbie invoicing a main contractor
  • Both VAT and CIS registered
  • Work is being sold on
  • No written end-user declaration

Normal VAT applies

  • Any domestic customer
  • Customer not VAT registered
  • Confirmed end user, in writing
  • Zero-rated new build work

The cash-flow consequence nobody warns you about

Before 2021, the VAT you charged sat in your account for up to three months and quietly funded your working capital. The reverse charge removed that float for every subbie whose sales are mostly business to business, while leaving input VAT on materials, fuel and plant fully reclaimable. The result: a large slice of the trade became permanent repayment traders, owed money by HMRC every single period.

Switch to monthly VAT returns

If you are consistently in a repayment position, monthly returns bring the refund forward twelve times a year instead of four. It is a form and a phone call, and for a busy subbie it is one of the largest free cash-flow improvements available.

Getting the paperwork right

  • Set each customer up correctly in software, once, so invoices default properly
  • Use the required wording: state the customer must account for the VAT
  • Show the VAT rate that would have applied, even though you are not charging it
  • Keep every end-user declaration on file with the job
  • Review new customers at onboarding, not at invoice time
