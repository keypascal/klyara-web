Markup is not margin: the arithmetic that quietly eats builders

29 Jun 2026 · 11 min read · by the Klyara team

30 second versionBreak it down for me

  • Markup is measured against cost; margin against price. They are never the same number.
  • Adding 20% markup gives you a 16.7% margin. For a 20% margin you must add 25%.
  • The formula: markup equals margin divided by one minus margin.
  • Correct markup on the wrong cost still loses money. Include overheads and unbilled hours in your day rate.

Here is the arithmetic that quietly costs the trade a fortune: adding 20% to your costs does not give you a 20% margin. It gives you 16.7. Do that all year on materials-heavy work and the gap between the margin you think you make and the margin you actually make runs to thousands.

The two words, made concrete

Markup is measured against your cost. Margin is measured against your price. Same money, different denominator, and the price is always the bigger number, so the margin is always the smaller percentage. A £1,000 cost plus 20% markup is a £1,200 price, and the £200 of profit is 16.7% of £1,200.

Apply that gap across a year of materials-heavy work and it is a holiday, a van deposit, or a tax bill.

The second half nobody costs

Even correct markup leaves you short if the cost you started from was wrong. The genuine cost of a day includes the overheads that arrive whether you work or not: van, insurance, phone, software, the yard, and the unbilled hours spent quoting and chasing. Divide your annual overheads by your realistic billable days and add that to every day you price. Firms that skip this are pricing against competitors who also skipped it, which is how an entire local market ends up underpriced.

Test it on your last three jobs

Take the final figures, not the quote. Subtract every real cost including your own time at a proper rate. If the answer is under ten percent, the problem is almost never that you worked too slowly.

Job-level books make that test a five-minute monthly habit instead of a project. See how job costing works.

Building a day rate that actually covers you

Correct markup applied to an incomplete cost is still a loss. The cost of a working day is not just your wages; it is every pound the business spends to make that day possible, spread across the days you can genuinely bill.

  1. 1
    Total your annual overheads

    Van and fuel, insurance, phone, software, accountancy, tools and replacement, PPE, yard or storage, advertising, bank charges, training, professional memberships.

  2. 2
    Count your realistic billable days

    52 weeks, minus holiday, bank holidays, sickness, weather days, and the days spent quoting, buying and chasing. For most working trades the answer lands between 180 and 210, not 260.

  3. 3
    Divide one by the other

    That is your overhead cost per billable day. It is usually higher than people expect, and it belongs in every price you quote.

  4. 4
    Add the wage you actually want

    Not what you scrape by on. The number that makes the work worth doing.

  5. 5
    Then add margin on top

    Margin is not your wage. It is the buffer that funds bad debts, warranty work, growth and the next van.

The mistake in the last row

To get a 20% margin you divide by 0.8, giving £368. Multiplying by 1.2 gives £353 and a margin of only 16.7%. Fifteen pounds a day, across 190 days, is nearly £3,000 a year of quiet loss.

Materials markup: the part customers query

Charging a margin on materials is normal, standard practice, and defensible: you fund them, you collect them, you carry the risk if they are wrong or damaged, and you warrant them. Ten to twenty percent is typical in the trade. The firms that get challenged on it are usually the ones who hide it and get caught, not the ones who state plainly that materials are supplied with a handling charge included.

Healthy pricing habits

  • Overheads costed into every day
  • Margin calculated with division, not multiplication
  • Materials carry a stated handling margin
  • Quoted margin compared against actual margin

Habits that leak money

  • Pricing from what the competition charges
  • Adding a percentage and calling it margin
  • Materials passed on at cost as a favour
  • Never checking the outcome against the quote
