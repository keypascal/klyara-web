VAT

Zero rated, five percent, or standard? VAT on new builds and conversions

20 Jul 2026 · 12 min read · by the Klyara team

30 second versionBreak it down for me

  • New build dwellings are zero rated; qualifying conversions and long-empty homes are five percent.
  • Ordinary extensions, refurbishments and repairs stay at twenty percent.
  • The evidence you hold is what lets you defend any rate below standard.
  • Wrong rate either costs your margin or leaves you owing HMRC. Only the right rate is free.

Most VAT-registered builders charge twenty percent on everything and move on. On new builds, conversions and certain renovations that is simply wrong, and it costs either your margin or your customer's goodwill, depending which way the error falls.

Qualifying conversions5%reduced rate, conditions apply
Most other work20%the standard rate, and the safe default only when it is correct

Simplified summary. The detail in HMRC's notices decides real cases, and the evidence you hold decides whether you can defend the rate you charged.

The margin arithmetic nobody runs

Charge twenty percent on a job that qualified for five and a savvy customer will find out, ask for it back, and you will be unwinding invoices. Charge five on a job that was standard rated and HMRC will want the difference from you, not from them. Both directions cost; only the right rate is free.

Evidence is the whole job

Keep this with every reduced or zero rated job

  • Planning permission or building control references confirming the nature of the works
  • Council tax or utility evidence for empty-property claims
  • A written certificate from the customer where the rules require one
  • Photographs of the property before works, dated
  • Your own note explaining which relief you applied and why

The reverse charge sits on top of all this for business-to-business work, which is where the trade's VAT gets genuinely fiddly. The reverse charge, explained plainly.

Zero rating: the conditions in practice

Zero rating on new dwellings is generous and specific. The building must be genuinely new, designed as a dwelling, with planning permission consistent with that use and no prohibition on separate disposal. Convert a barn and you are usually at five percent, not zero. Build a granny annexe that cannot be sold separately and zero rating typically fails. The distinctions sound pedantic until an assessment arrives.

Materials follow the labour, sometimes

On zero and reduced rated work, materials supplied with your services generally take the same rate, but only where they are 'building materials' as defined and are incorporated into the building. Carpets and most free-standing appliances are excluded, which surprises people fitting kitchens.

The reverse charge, layered on top

For business-to-business construction work between VAT-registered and CIS-registered parties, the domestic reverse charge shifts the VAT accounting to the customer, and your invoice shows no VAT at all with the required wording. It does not change the underlying rate; it changes who accounts for it. Where the customer is an end user or an intermediary supplier and confirms that in writing, normal VAT applies instead.

  1. 1
    Establish the rate first

    New build, conversion, or standard work: get the rate right before considering who accounts for it.

  2. 2
    Then establish the customer type

    VAT registered, CIS registered, and not an end user? Reverse charge applies.

  3. 3
    Get end user confirmation in writing

    No written confirmation and the other conditions met means reverse charge, not a judgement call.

  4. 4
    Set the customer up correctly in software

    Per contact, so every invoice for that customer defaults correctly.

  5. 5
    Consider monthly VAT returns

    If most of your sales are reverse charge, you are likely a repayment trader and monthly returns turn that into monthly cash.

Charge the wrong rate and either your margin pays for it or HMRC does, and only one of those two will let it go.
