MTD and tax

Should you go limited? The real numbers for subbies

22 Jul 2026 · 12 min read · by the Klyara team

30 second versionBreak it down for me

  • A company is a separate legal person: limited liability, different tax, more admin.
  • The crossover usually sits somewhere above 40,000 to 50,000 pounds of profit, and it moves every Budget.
  • CIS still applies to companies, with deductions offset through payroll.
  • Company money is not your money: drawings become dividends, salary or a director's loan.

"You want to go limited, mate" is handed out on site like biscuits. Sometimes it is right. Here is the actual decision, with the myths removed.

What you gain

Limited liability: the company's debts are the company's, which matters more the bigger the jobs get. Tax planning room: Corporation Tax runs at 19 percent on small profits (25 with marginal relief above 50,000), and you choose the mix of salary and dividends you draw. At healthy profits this beats sole-trader Income Tax plus Class 4 National Insurance. Credibility: some main contractors and commercial clients simply prefer dealing with a company.

What you take on

Statutory accounts and a Corporation Tax return, filed by an accountant, every year. Companies House filings, and now identity verification for every director. Payroll for yourself. A separate bank account and the discipline that the company's money is not your money. Realistically, 1,000 pounds or more a year in extra compliance cost, plus admin you will feel.

The honest threshold

Below roughly 30,000 pounds of profit, the savings rarely clear the extra cost and hassle; sole trader keeps life simple, and MTD applies to you either way. From around 40,000 to 50,000 and climbing, the numbers usually start to favour a company, and the liability protection sweetens it. In between sits a judgement call about growth, risk and how much admin you can stomach.

One warning

Going limited under CIS resets practical things: contractors must verify the company, gross status must be applied for again, and mixing personal and company money undoes every benefit. Do it as a plan, not a pub decision. We will run your numbers free, and if the answer is "stay sole trader", that is what we will say.

What actually changes when you incorporate

Sole traderLimited company
Legal statusYou are the businessA separate legal person
LiabilityPersonal, unlimitedLimited to the company, with director duties
TaxIncome tax and Class 4 NI on profitCorporation Tax, then tax on salary and dividends
Taking moneyDrawings, freelySalary, dividends, or a director's loan
CISDeductions credited on your returnDeductions offset through payroll in-year
FilingsOne return a yearAccounts, CT600, confirmation statement, payroll
PrivacyNothing publicDirectors, accounts and PSCs on the public register
PerceptionFine domesticallyOften expected by commercial clients

Tax is only one column of eight. Plenty of firms incorporate for liability and credibility rather than the tax arithmetic.

Company money is not your money

This is the change that catches people. Taking cash out casually creates a director's loan with tax consequences. Salary and dividends need to be planned and documented. The discipline is not hard, but it is not optional.

Before you incorporate

  • Run the numbers on your actual profit, not a rule of thumb from the pub
  • Factor in the extra admin cost: accounts, CT600, payroll, confirmation statement
  • Check whether your customers care: some commercial clients only contract with companies
  • Understand director duties and Companies House identity verification
  • Plan how you will pay yourself, and set the standing rules before the first month

Incorporating usually makes sense when

  • Profit comfortably clears the crossover
  • You want liability protection
  • Commercial clients expect it
  • You are reinvesting rather than drawing everything

Staying a sole trader when

  • Profit is modest and stable
  • Admin capacity is limited
  • Work is domestic and referral-driven
  • You need every pound out of the business each month
