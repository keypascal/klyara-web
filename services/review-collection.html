Fair questions,
straight answers.

Yes: asking customers for honest reviews is legal, encouraged by Google, and simply how the modern reputation game is played: what is NOT allowed, and what we never do, is the dirty stuff around the edges: paying for reviews, faking them, or review-gating (asking only the customers you think will be positive, filtering the rest to a private feedback form): Google's policies and UK consumer law both prohibit it, detection is real, and the penalty (reviews wiped, profile suppressed) lands on YOUR name, not the agency that suggested it. Our system asks every completed customer, at the moment satisfaction peaks (job done, site tidy, invoice sent), with one polite text and one gentle nudge: no pressure, no scripts telling customers what to write, no incentives. The maths works without cheating: most happy customers simply never think to review, and the entire trick is asking properly at the right moment. Firms with hundreds of five-stars did not have hundreds of ecstatic customers: they asked, consistently, for years: which is now a service you can switch on.
First, breathe: a considered response to a bad review does more for your business than three more five-stars, because the real audience is the hundreds of future customers reading how you handle criticism, not the one unhappy reviewer. The playbook we run within one working day: acknowledge specifically (no copy-paste corporate sorry), own what is genuinely yours, state the fix, and move it offline ('call me directly and we will sort it'): done in your voice, drafted for your one-tap approval or sent on your standing instruction. What we talk you out of: the 3am rebuttal essay, the airing of the customer's own sins, the lawyer threat: all screenshot fuel that converts one bad review into a local Facebook event. Where a review is unfair, respond gracefully anyway AND pursue removal in parallel (next question). One bad review in a stream of genuine five-stars reads as reassuring proof the reviews are real: which, statistically, it is. The engine's volume is your armour: velocity makes any single opinion boring.
Sometimes, honestly: Google removes reviews that breach its policies: fake ones (no customer relationship: the competitor's cousin, the wrong business entirely), spam, conflicts of interest, hate speech, off-topic rants about parking: and we pursue those through the reporting process with evidence, winning a respectable share, especially the wrong-business and never-a-customer cases. What nobody can remove is a genuine customer's genuine opinion, however unfair it feels, and any agency promising guaranteed removals is selling you a lie with a monthly fee attached: the industry is full of them and they deliver strongly-worded emails and nothing. Where removal fails, the response strategy (previous answer) and volume strategy (steady genuine five-stars) do the actual work: a 4.8 with two hundred reviews is bulletproof against anything one angry Tuesday can produce. Legal routes exist for genuinely defamatory fabrications and we will point you to the right professional for those rare cases. Everything else is patience, grace in public, and arithmetic.
Google first and it is not close: Google reviews power the map pack where local trade searches actually convert, they surface beside your name in every search, and they feed the ranking signals the SEO work depends on: the default setting sends every review request there until your Google base is strong. Then it depends on your work: Checkatrade and Trustpilot where homeowners in your area actually check them, Facebook recommendations because local group word-of-mouth lives there and a page with recommendations converts the lurkers. Trade-platform reviews (Checkatrade, MyBuilder) matter enormously IF you pay for those memberships: the system routes a share of requests there to keep those profiles alive. What we avoid: spraying requests across six platforms until none reaches critical mass: concentration wins, and the monthly report shows exactly where your next ten reviews should aim. Platform mix is a setting, reviewed quarterly as your presence matures: start concentrated, diversify from strength.
Not as we send them: one polite message at the natural moment (job complete, everyone happy), one gentle reminder a few days later for the majority who meant to and forgot, then silence: nobody gets a third message, ever, and any reply of 'no thanks' stops the sequence instantly and permanently for that customer. The tone is yours, drafted in your voice ('Cheers for having us in, Dave: if you were happy with the bathroom, a quick Google review helps a small firm massively: link here'), not marketing-speak, and the ask lands as what it is: a small favour to a tradesperson they liked. Response rates say customers do not mind: a healthy share click through, and complaints are statistically negligible when the moment and tone are right: annoyance is a symptom of bad timing or robot-voice, both of which are configuration, both of which we tune. You approve the exact wording before anything sends. The alternative: awkwardly asking in person at handover, forgetting, or not asking at all: is how firms with twenty years of happy customers end up with nine reviews.

The engine adapts: for commercial clients the ask pivots from Google reviews to testimonials, case-study permissions and LinkedIn recommendations: the currencies that actually circulate in B2B: while any domestic or small-commercial work you do still feeds the public review base. Velocity that matches your job flow reads as exactly what it is: a busy firm whose customers are happy.
