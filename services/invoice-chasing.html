The evidence says the opposite, and here is why: the sequences are firm but scrupulously courteous, and crucially they come from the bookkeeper, not from you, which depersonalises the whole exchange. 'Klyara, on behalf of Dave's Brickwork' chasing an invoice is process; Dave chasing it at 9pm is personal, and both sides feel the difference. Most late payment is disorganisation rather than malice: the polite day-one reminder catches the genuinely-forgot crowd (a surprising share), and the escalating rhythm sorts the rest without a single awkward phone call from you. You will lose zero good customers to a professional reminder, because good customers pay; several clients report the opposite effect, that customers treat them more professionally once invoices clearly have a system behind them. The relationships you might strain are precisely the ones straining your cashflow. And you approve the tone tiers at setup, so nothing goes out sharper than you are comfortable with.
Yes, per customer, per invoice, or per rule, and everyone uses this: the main contractor you cannot afford to irritate mid-negotiation, the customer with a bereavement, your brother-in-law. Exclusions take one tap in the portal or one text to us, and excluded invoices still appear on your radar so opting someone out of chasing never means forgetting they owe you. You can also set customer-specific rhythms: gentle-only for the fragile relationships, standard for trade, accelerated for the serial offenders who only respond to the statutory-interest letter. Some clients run a whitelist model where chasing is default-on except named accounts; others default-off and enable per invoice. The system bends to your relationships rather than steamrolling them: the automation is the engine, but you hold the wheel.
Disputes get pulled out of the chasing sequence the moment they are flagged, because chasing a disputed invoice is how you turn a resolvable disagreement into a grudge. The invoice moves to a disputed status, the automatic rhythm stops, and it lands on your radar with the correspondence attached so the actual disagreement (snagging, a variation never signed off, a materials price) can be dealt with on its merits. Once resolved, one tap resumes the sequence from a sensible point, usually a fresh polite note reflecting whatever was agreed. We also see the pattern data: a customer who 'disputes' every third invoice on a new pretext each time is not disputing, they are financing their business with your patience, and the reporting makes that visible so you can decide about future work with open eyes. The system's job is to make sure genuine disputes get human judgement and fake ones stop working.
We take it to the door of formal action and hand you over well-armed, rather than pretending to be solicitors: the sequence's final stages include the statutory late-payment letter (the one citing the Late Payment of Commercial Debts legislation with interest and compensation calculated to the penny) and a formal letter before action if you choose. Beyond that, we prepare the pack that makes the next step cheap and strong: the full invoice history, every reminder sent and delivered, the dispute log, the interest calculation. Money claims online for straightforward debts is something many clients then do themselves in an evening with that pack; for bigger or messier debts we introduce a solicitor or a reputable collection agency and you deal with them directly. What we never do is surprise your customer with a debt collector you did not authorise. The honest truth: the pack rarely gets used, because a customer who has watched a competent, documented sequence unfold knows exactly how a judge would read it.

Retention balances are tracked separately with their own release-date-driven reminders, which is a different rhythm from ordinary debt (chasing a retention early annoys people; forgetting it donates the money). Applications-for-payment cycles, where the paperwork rhythm matters as much as the amount, are supported with date-driven rather than invoice-driven chasing.
